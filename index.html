Per what? Rain, darkness, and the number 7.7 million crashes do not contain

Most reported crashes in the United States happen in clear weather, on dry roads, in daylight. Read as risk, that is an argument for driving in the rain. Physics says the opposite. This page sets the two against each other and shows why both are true.

The physics of stopping

simulation

From the moment a hazard appears, a car travels while the driver reacts (v·tr) and then while it brakes (v²/2μg). The first grows with speed. The second grows with speed squared and with less friction. Both bars share one axis in feet.

reaction distancebraking, dry roadbraking, wet road

Assumptions. Constant deceleration through the whole stop, so μ is fixed. Level road. One braking input, no steering. One reaction time, not a distribution. g = 32.174 ft/s². Typical μ: about 0.70 dry asphalt, 0.40 wet. AASHTO's design reaction time is 2.5 s. This is a model, not a measurement of any crash.

The denominator the data does not have

reported datayour assumption

A count is exposure times risk. If 8% of crashes happen in rain, that is alarming only if far less than 8% of driving happens in rain. The dataset cannot say which. You supply the missing number: drag the slider and watch which line is on top.

Rates are relative to the average crash rate across both conditions, so 1.0 means "average". Near zero exposure the condition's rate runs off the chart: dividing by almost nothing produces a dramatic number that means nothing, so that region is clamped and labelled rather than drawn.

Data, model and method

Data

US Accidents (2016 to 2023), Sobhan Moosavi et al., about 7.7 million reported incidents across the contiguous US, 46 columns, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. These are incidents relayed by traffic APIs, not a crash census. Kaggle source.

Model

d = v·tr + v²/(2μg), with v in ft/s, tr in s, μ dimensionless and g = 32.174 ft/s². Checked against AASHTO's published stopping sight distance: at 60 mph with tr = 2.5 s and a = 3.4 m/s² (μ = 0.347) the model gives 567 ft against a published 570.
